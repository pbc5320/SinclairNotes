1The three Cs

The title names the toolkit. Each C feeds the next: you build a corpus, view it through concordances, and use them to spot collocations.

C

Corpus

A large, principled collection of real texts stored electronically: news, conversation, fiction, academic writing. It replaces invented example sentences with what people actually wrote and said.

Many texts, one searchable body of evidence.
C

Concordance

A display of every occurrence of a search word, lined up in the centre with its surrounding context. The format is called KWIC: Key Word In Context.

…fear that rot set in before…
…once despair set in among…
…and panic set in as the…
C

Collocation

The tendency of words to occur near each other more often than chance would predict. It is habit, not grammar rules.

✓ heavy rain
✗ strong rain
✓ strong tea
✗ heavy tea

2Two ways to build language

Sinclair's most influential move: two principles of how speakers put language together. Toggle to compare them.

Slot 1The manager
a storm · she · Ravi…
Slot 2announced
denied · wrote · sang…
Slot 3the plan
a rule · the news…
Slot 4yesterday
in May · loudly…

The textbook picture. Each slot is filled independently by any word the grammar allows. Language is a huge set of single-word choices.

as a matter of fact

Closer to real use. Speakers often select a ready-made or semi-fixed phrase in one move. Sinclair argues this idiom principle covers far more of ordinary language than traditional grammar and dictionaries recognised, and you can only see it in large amounts of data.

4Toward semantic prosody

Collocation is the door. Behind it, Sinclair's later work describes a word's behaviour as layers of an extended unit of meaning. Click each layer, shown with set in as the running example.

Where the term comes from. In the 1991 book Sinclair observes that some words keep a particular kind of company, for example set in and happen attracting unpleasant subjects. The label semantic prosody was later coined by Louw (1993), borrowing "prosody" from Firth's prosodic phonology, where a feature spreads across a stretch of speech. Sinclair subsequently framed it as the part of a unit of meaning that expresses the speaker's attitude or purpose. Check exact wording and page references in your own copies before citing.

Examples widely cited in the literature

rot, decay, despair, infection

happen

problems, damage, death, concern
unpleasantStubbs 1995

Treat these as hypotheses to test in your own corpora, not as settled facts. A word's prosody can differ by corpus, register, and period.

5A workflow for your research

A starting scaffold for a corpus study of semantic prosody. Part II gives you a worksheet for each step.

Frame the question

Pick node word(s) and an angle: one word's prosody, near-synonyms compared, or one word across corpora.

Choose and justify your corpora

Size, register, time period, balance. Prosody is always relative to the corpus you sampled.

Extract concordances and collocates

Set a span (e.g. ±4 or ±5), a minimum frequency, and an association measure.

MIt-scorelog-likelihoodlogDice

Group collocates by meaning

Sort collocates into semantic sets (semantic preference), for example states of deterioration or emotions.

Judge the prosody with a coding scheme

Hand-code a sample of lines for the attitude or evaluation they convey. Write a coding manual and, if possible, check agreement with a second coder.

Check the wider context and report

Look beyond the span at the clause and discourse level, then report proportions, examples, and your criteria.

Watch-outs to plan for
  • Association measures differ: MI favours rare, exclusive pairings, while t-score favours frequent ones.
  • "Positive" and "negative" are judgments. Define them, and consider whether you are coding word polarity or the attitude the whole line expresses (Hunston 2007 discusses this).
  • Irony, negation, and quotation can flip what a line does, so verify manually.
  • The concept itself has critics (e.g. Whitsitt 2005), so expect to defend how you operationalise it.

Quick self-check

Why is "strong rain" odd if it is grammatical?

Collocation is about habitual word partnership, not grammatical well-formedness. Speakers overwhelmingly say "heavy rain".

Which principle does "as a matter of fact" illustrate?

The idiom principle: a semi-fixed unit chosen as one piece rather than assembled slot by slot.

How do collocation and semantic prosody differ?

Collocation is which words co-occur. Semantic prosody is the attitude or evaluative colouring that the pattern of collocates gives the node word, such as a consistently unpleasant environment.

Why does your choice of corpus matter?

Prosody is a tendency observed in a sample. Different registers, periods, and balances can yield different tendencies, so the corpus must be justified and described.
